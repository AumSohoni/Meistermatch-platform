This report describes the research, design, implementation and early testing of 
MeisterMatch, a mobile -first reciprocal human -AI recruitment platform for skilled 
trades (electricians, plumbers, welders, carpenters and HVAC technicians) in Latvia 
and the Baltic region ( Hazans, 2019; European Labour Authority, 2026). It addresses 
the mismatch between text-dense job boards and informal hiring: personal referrals 
remain a common and effective way to fill vacancies in developed labour markets 
(Hoffman, 2017), but such matches leave neither side with an inspectable, data -
backed record. 
MeisterMatch combines a gesture -driven swipe -card interface with a readable 
multi-factor scoring engine whose factor weights can be adjusted from recruiter 
feedback. To align with Regulation ( EU) 2024/1689 (European Parliament & 
Council, 2024a) and Directive ( EU) 2024/2831 (CMS Law, 2024), it keeps a person 
in the decision loop. Rankings list plain match reasons, and recruiter feedback adjusts 
the factor weights through uncertainty sampling (Amershi et al., 2019). 
The prototype has a Python/FastAPI ranker, a Node.js/Express API and a React 
18/Vite frontend. Chapter 4 verifies that all 13 API endpoints run, that types stay 
consistent, that passwords are stored as salted scrypt hashes and that session tokens 
expire, and that the seed data ( 30 workers, 20 jobs) flows end to end. Chapter 5 
reports an executed controlled experiment, over 80 replicate markets at three market 
sizes, that compares the active -learning ranker with the fixed -weight baseline on 
Precision@K and NDCG; it finds a clear gain on the larger markets and no 
distinguishable gain on the smallest. Voice onboarding, calendar scheduling and 
messaging bots are out of scope.

including the title page, the list of references and the appendices; 11 tables, 3 figures, 
36 references.

INTRODUCTION 
Latvia, and the Baltics more broadly, keep running short of vocational workers, 
and this has been the case for years. Skilled trades occupations, including certified 
electricians, plumbers, industrial welders, carpenters and HVAC technicians, are 
among the hardest roles to fill in construction, manufacturing and municipal 
infrastructure. Older job sites (such as CV -Online, CV Market and Visidarbi.lv) rely 
on lengthy text descriptions, complex filter matrices and asynchronous resume 
uploads. These conventional interfaces serve the skilled trades sector poorly, because 
trades workers rely on informal peer networks, prioritise immediate local availability 
and wage transparency, and show low engagement with desktop applicant -tracking 
systems. 
AI screening tools promised to help by ranking CVs automatically. On their 
own, however, they are hard to inspect, they can repeat historical biases and they 
clash with emerging EU rules. While algorithmic screening can save time, 
autonomous decision-making brings risks of black -box opacity, reinforced historical 
bias and regulatory non -compliance ( Rigotti & Fosch-Villaronga, 2024). Under 
Regulation ( EU) 2024/1689 (the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act), AI 
applications in recruitment are designated as high -risk systems requiring human 
oversight, transparency and data governance. 
This report addresses these challenges by designing, building and checking 
MeisterMatch, a reciprocal human -AI job -matching platform for skilled -trades 
recruitment in Latvia (Hazans, 2019; European Labour Authority, 2026). 
The goal of the bachelor thesis is formulated as follows: to design, implement 
and verify a prototype of a reciprocal human -AI skilled -trades recruitment platform 
for the Latvian labour market, featuring a gesture -driven mobile interface, an 
interpretable multi -factor ranking engine, an active -learning feedback loop, and 
design consistent with EU human-oversight and transparency requirements for high -
risk AI applications (European Parliament & Council, 2024a). 
The tasks of the thesis are: (1) literature analysis, (2) hybrid architecture design, 
(3) architectural specification, ( 4) prototype implementation, ( 5) active -learning 
integration, (6) software verification and ( 7) evaluation methodology. Mutual interest 
is established strictly through dual consent, so contact details are unlocked only when 
both candidate and employer express interest. A stated trust limitation is that informal

extension, with a preliminary run executed and reported in Chapter 5.

1. LABOUR MARKET CHALLENGES AND THESIS 
OBJECTIVES 
1.1. Statement of the Problem 
The Latvian labour market has a clear supply -demand gap in vocational 
technical trades. Studies of Latvian emigration ( Hazans, 2019) show that emigration, 
peaking after the 2008-2009 crisis, removed a large share of the working -age 
population from manual, craft and technical trades: between 2008 and 2017 about 
260,000 people left the country, equivalent to 13.5% of the resident population in 
2017 (OECD, 2019a). This outflow has contributed to persistent shortages in skilled 
trades that education and VET reforms have so far not offset (Cedefop, 2024). 
According to official labour-market forecasts published by the Latvian Ministry 
of Economics ( 2020), Latvia will face a shortage of around 40,000 workers with 
secondary vocational qualifications in the medium term, alongside a persistent surplus 
of low-skilled workers. The EURES report of the European Labour Authority ( 2026) 
lists craft workers, building electricians, welders and plasterers among the widespread 
shortage occupations in Latvia; roofers and plumbers are not reported as shortage 
occupations for Latvia, although they are reported as shortages elsewhere in the EU. 
Traditional online portals only partly resolve this gap, because trades recruitment in 
Latvia still relies heavily on personal networks and referrals ( Hoffman, 2017). Search 
frictions of this kind are precisely the inefficiency that electronic labour -market 
matching is meant to reduce ( Autor, 2001), yet the design of most job boards is ill -
suited to this segment. 
The shortage is felt most acutely by small and medium enterprises, which 
dominate construction, manufacturing and property maintenance in Latvia and rarely 
have dedicated recruitment staff: vacancy postings are created manually, applications 
arrive asynchronously, and shortlisting relies on memory and informal reputation 
rather than structured data. On the worker side, certified tradespeople hold several 
active skills and move between engagements of varying length, so a browsable profile 
that states availability and an hourly rate is more useful than a date -stamped 
application. These observations drive the interface and ranker choices made in 
Chapter 3.

1.4. Scope Boundaries and De-Scoped Features 
A bachelor thesis cannot cover everything, so the scope was kept narrow. The

automated Telegram notification bots, were excluded from the assessed prototype and

urgent on -demand dispatch (a client needing a plumber immediately). This thesis 
instead covers longer-term reciprocal hiring through mutual swiping; urgent two -hour 
dispatch is explicitly out of scope.

• User-experience alignment: gesture -driven mobile interfaces lower friction for

The analysis of information sources draws on peer -reviewed academic 
literature, official labour -market statistics, industry reports, technical documentation 
and EU policy documents. Sources were identified through Google Scholar, IEEE 
Xplore, the ACM Digital Library, Crossref and official statistical and regulatory 
portals. The sections below summarise the findings by thematic area: skilled -trades

2.1. Skilled Trades Recruitment Dynamics in Latvia and the Baltics 
The local picture is based on EU Labour Force Survey and regional labour -
market studies ( Hazans, 2019; OECD, 2019a; OECD, 2019b). Professional white -
collar hiring relies on commercial job portals (CV -Online, CV Market), whereas 
skilled-trades recruitment leans on informal networks, personal recommendations and 
local classified portals (for example ss.lv / ss.com). OECD evaluations find that 
registered vacancies account for less than 20% of all hires ( OECD, 2019b), and 
referral hiring lowers recruiting costs and turnover in developed markets, though it 
can reinforce homophily ( Hoffman, 2017). In Latvian practice (European Labour 
Authority, 2026), informal networks dominate trades recruitment because they 
provide peer trust and verification of practical craftsmanship, which static CV text 
fails to communicate. 
The mismatch is structural rather than cyclical. The post-2008 emigration wave 
removed experienced manual workers faster than the domestic education system 
could replace them, and vocational enrolment never fully recovered ( Hazans, 2019; 
OECD, 2019a). Because trades work is location -bound and often requires Latvian or 
Russian language competency, international replacement talent is only a partial 
answer, which strengthens the case for better domestic matching rather than 
additional supply alone (European Labour Authority, 2026). The economic logic of 
lowering search frictions in such a market is well established: electronic matching 
=== PAGE 13 ===
13 
raises the efficiency of vacancy-candidate contact even when the underlying skills gap 
persists (Autor, 2001). 
At the EU level the signals are consistent: the European Labour Authority's 
shortage report confirms that craft workers, building electricians, welders and 
plasterers are among the occupations with recognised shortages in Latvia (European 
Labour Authority, 2026). Because EURES covers only formally reported vacancies, 
these counts under -state informal hiring, so combining EURES data with labour -
force-survey evidence and the referral literature gives a more complete picture 
(Hazans, 2019; OECD, 2019b). Two implications follow: the scarcity of trades labour 
is a matching problem as much as a supply problem, so improving contact efficiency 
is an immediately actionable contribution ( Autor, 2001); and a tool for this market 
must expect many economically active candidates who are not browsing vacancy 
boards, which motivates the push-oriented card deck proposed here. 
2.2. Hybrid Human-AI Architectures and Algorithmic Matching 
The introduction of algorithmic recommendation engines into labour 
marketplaces affects matching efficiency. In a large -scale field experiment on a 
digital labour market, Horton (2017) showed that providing employers with 
algorithmically generated candidate recommendations increased job fill rates for 
technical vacancies by 20% compared with control groups, without crowding out 
organic non -recommended applicants. Because the experiment ran in an online 
freelance market rather than in Latvian skilled -trades recruitment, external validity is 
limited: such recommendations should be read as complementing, not substituting for, 
costly manual screening. 
Fully automated decision systems, however, introduce risks of systematic bias 
and opacity. Statistical discrimination can arise even from accurate proxy variables, 
so disparate impact is a structural property of data -driven screening rather than an 
isolated bug ( Barocas & Selbst, 2016). In digital work settings more broadly, 
algorithmic control reshapes the relationship between platforms and workers and is 
contested by the people subject to it ( Kellogg et al., 2020). To mitigate these risks, 
MeisterMatch adopts the hybrid-intelligence framework formulated by Dellermann et 
al. ( 2019). Hybrid intelligence combines complementary human cognitive abilities 
(contextual judgment, ethics, domain intuition) with machine capabilities (rapid 
pattern recognition, multi -criteria scoring). By embedding recruiters directly in the 
=== PAGE 14 ===
14 
decision loop, the system functions as a supportive recommender rather than an 
autonomous decision -maker. These interaction principles follow Amershi et al. 
(2019). 
Within the hybrid-intelligence view, the division of labour must be explicit. The 
machine contributes consistency: it applies the same scoring rules to every pair and 
quantifies each factor. The human contributes context: whether an employer replies, 
whether a candidate is reachable, whether a borderline score is worth an interview. 
This keeps the system defensible under the EU AI Act, because the recommendation 
is an input to a human decision rather than the decision itself (European Parliament & 
Council, 2024a). Following the interaction guidelines of Amershi et al. ( 2019), each 
deck card renders the factor bars and plain -language reasons, so a recruiter can agree 
with, override or refine a ranking - the first step toward the active -learning feedback 
described in Section 2.3 and evaluated in Section 5.7. 
2.3. Active-Learning Feedback Mechanisms 
Active learning here means the following. The build records recruiter likes and 
passes (like = 1, pass = 0) and fits the ranker’s factor weights to that feedback. The 
learning layer is a logistic regression on the five factor scores (skill overlap, district 
match, availability match, pay fit and experience): a weighted sum, squashed by the 
logistic function, supplies a calibrated probability, and each feedback batch performs 
one ridge -regularised update of the weights by iteratively reweighted least squares. 
Sampling then presents unseen pairs whose predicted probability is closest to 0.50 
(maximum uncertainty). This follows the active -learning paradigm in which the 
learner queries the most informative points (Settles, 2012). 
Updates are evaluated on labels the learner never trains on. Each replicate holds 
out an evaluation share of the market pairs and a validation share of the feedback; the 
evaluation share scores Precision@K and NDCG, and the validation share triggers a 
rollback that reverts an update whenever validation NDCG drops more than a fixed 
margin below its best value. Ridge regularisation toward the starting weights and a 
bounded weight corridor limit how far one batch can move a factor, and the learner 
only adjusts factor weights, never the trade filter or the pay and availability rules that 
encode hard requirements. Active preference elicitation reaches higher accuracy from 
fewer interactions than passive rating collection ( Elahi et al., 2016; Rubens et al., 
2015), while multilingual models such as XLM-R (Conneau et al., 2020; Hu et al., 
=== PAGE 15 ===
15 
2020) remain a candidate feature extractor for the final stage only and are not 
connected to the current ranker. 
2.4. Mobile Gesture UX, Reciprocal Matching and Behavioural 
Dynamics 
Gesture-driven swipe interactions for job matching were operationalised by 
Raghavendra & Chitra ( 2025) in SwipeRight, where combining swiping with real -
time state synchronisation shortened screening cycles and increased engagement. 
Reciprocal models score candidate-job pairs from both sides of the market (Yao et al., 
2023), and reciprocal recommender systems more generally model mutual rather than 
one-sided preference ( Palomares et al., 2021). Palomares et al. distinguish score -
based methods, which compute a utility for each side and combine them, from 
collaborative and content -based methods. MeisterMatch belongs to the score -based 
family: jobs-for-worker and workers-for-job scoring are computed independently, and 
mutual interest comes from the independent like signals rather than a single combined 
utility, so neither side's score reveals the other's. The platform is a two -sided market 
with the network effects and cold -start dynamics that platform economics describes 
(Rochet & Tirole, 2003; Schein et al., 2002). 
On the behavioural side, large, undifferentiated choice sets can undermine 
motivation (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000). Waleson (2022) compared fast card -swiping 
with slow browsing in a mock dating app and found no significant wellbeing effect, 
so whether curated, pre-ranked decks prevent choice overload in job search remains a 
hypothesis. Gamified mechanics such as instant match feedback are widely used to 
motivate candidates ( Obaid et al., 2020). To allow early testing despite network 
sparsity, the prototype uses deterministic synthetic seed profiles ( 30 workers, 20 
jobs); this follows the general bootstrapping pattern discussed by Jobtech Alliance 
(2026), not their recommendation of these exact numbers. 
2.5. EU Legal Compliance, Data Privacy and Algorithmic 
Governance 
Because EU rules apply to hiring AI, the design includes the following controls: 
• EU AI Act (European Parliament & Council, 2024a): under Regulation ( EU) 
2024/1689, AI systems used in recruitment and employment are classified 
=== PAGE 16 ===
16 
as high -risk (Annex III). The law mandates risk management, data 
governance, transparency and human oversight. 
• Platform Work Directive (European Parliament & Council, 2024b; CMS Law, 
2024): applies to digital labour platforms and to persons performing 
platform work; its relevance here depends on whether matchmaking counts 
as algorithmic management, which is an open legal question. Its limits on 
processing sensitive personal data are narrower than a general ban. Related 
GDPR Art. 22 safeguards on solely automated decisions are also 
considered in the design. 
• GDPR compliance: in a systematic literature review, Amaral Cejas et al. 
(2026) note that mobile apps frequently violate data -subject rights (access, 
erasure, consent withdrawal) . MeisterMatch implements strict data 
minimisation and user-controlled profile visibility. 
Human review is not claimed to guarantee fairness, because human recruiters 
also possess cognitive biases ( Köchling & Wehner, 2020; Raghavan et al., 2020). 
Human review provides a mechanism to inspect, contest and override 
recommendations; it does not remove bias on its own. 
2.6. Related Marketplaces for Skilled-Trades Work 
A cross-sectional look at existing consumer-facing marketplaces for trades work 
positions MeisterMatch relative to commercial practice. Platforms such as Hipages 
(Australia), MyBuilder (United Kingdom) and Thumbtack (United States) are service-
sourcing marketplaces: a household posts a job, vetted professionals respond with 
quotes, and the platform mediates trust through reviews and insurance. From a 
matching-theory viewpoint they are one-sided response markets rather than reciprocal 
markets, because the household selects among responders while the professional's 
decision problem (which jobs to pursue, at what price) stays implicit. They are also 
quote-based and transaction -heavy, which suits one -off household repairs less well 
than the continuous or project -based staffing relationships that construction and 
municipal employers in Latvia need (Hazans, 2019). 
Two-sided recruitment for trade labour, by contrast, resembles the graduate -
referral matching of Yao et al. (2023), in which both sides swipe and a match requires 
mutual interest, or the swipe mechanics of Raghavendra & Chitra ( 2025). 
MeisterMatch occupies this category: a hiring tool for employers who already need 
=== PAGE 17 ===
17 
electricians and welders, not a lead -generation board. The unit of value is a staffing 
relationship rather than a quotation, so the platform emphasises stated availability, 
hourly rates and district proximity. To the author's knowledge, no current platform 
combines all four properties investigated here - reciprocal dual-consent matching, an 
interpretable multi-factor ranker, an active-learning feedback loop and alignment with 
EU high-risk AI obligations - which is the gap this work aims to close.

2.8. Results of the Analytical Part 
The analytical literature review produced four findings that shaped the system 
design: 
1. Informal local dynamics: skilled-trades recruitment in Latvia leans heavily on 
informal networks and personal referrals ( Hoffman, 2017), which is why 
this thesis explores a mobile -first, low -friction interface as one possible 
way to reduce interaction barriers. 
2. Recommender liquidity: algorithmic recommendations increase job fill rates 
by 20% for technical vacancies in online labour markets (Horton, 2017), 
=== PAGE 18 ===

but must preserve human oversight under the EU AI Act (European 
Parliament & Council, 2024a). 
3. Active feedback efficiency: active learning with uncertainty sampling 
optimises feature weights under sparse feedback constraints (Settles, 2012; 
Elahi et al., 2016). 
4. Gesture UX benefits: choice overload is a documented risk when options are 
numerous and undifferentiated (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000); whether pre -

(Waleson, 2022 found no wellbeing effect of swiping speed). 
The analysis also confirmed a design implication from platform economics: as a 
two-sided market, MeisterMatch must solve a cold-start problem on both sides, which

3.3. Matching Engine Implementation 
The matching engine ( ml/ranker.py) calculates candidate -job relevance using a 
transparent point-based scoring function over five factors, with a hard trade filter that 
removes non-matching trades before scoring. The five factors are skill overlap, same -
district geography, availability, pay compatibility and experience. Fig. 3.2 shows the 
maximum points each factor can contribute. 
 
Fig. 3.2. Maximum Points Contributed by Each Interpretable Scoring Factor 
Points are awarded per factor: skill contributes up to 50 points ( 50 times the 
number of matched required skills divided by the number of required skills; 25 points 
if the job lists no required skills); same -district geography adds 15; availability adds 
10 for an exact availability match or 4 if either side is immediate; pay within the 
employer’s budget adds 15 (or 8 if the rate is within 10%); and experience adds 10 for 
5 or more years, 5 for at least 1 year and 2 otherwise. The total is capped at 100. 
There is no language factor in the current build, because job posts do not carry 
required languages; language profiles exist on worker records only. A limitation is 
that the geographic factor currently covers ten seeded Riga districts ( Centrs, 
Purvciems, Agenskalns, Imanta, Jugla, Kengarags, Plavnieki, Sarkandaugava, Teika, 
Ziepniekkalns); Latgale, Kurzeme and Vidzeme are not yet modelled, which limits 
the peripheral-region motivation and is left for the remaining work. 
The geographic factor is a deliberate simplification. Proximity is a binary same-
district signal: identical districts add 15 points and any different -district pair adds 
none, so a pair across two adjacent districts scores exactly like a pair on opposite 

=== PAGE 22 ===
22 
sides of the city. A finer proximity model would act on a limited signal in the current 
market, because the executed experiment of Section 5.7 recovers a district sensitivity 
in the lower half of the five factors, below availability and pay; such a model is left to 
future work. 
Worked example. For an electrician in Purvciems with 3 years of experience, 
availability in one -to-two weeks and a rate of 20 EUR/h, matched against a Centrs 
electrician job requiring Wiring, HVAC and PLC, starting immediately and paying

3.4. Authentication and Session Model 
Authentication follows a token -based design with hashed credentials and 
expiring sessions. The API exposes four auth routes: register, login, logout and a 
current-user route. On register, the username and password are validated (the 
username must be at least three characters), the password is stored as a salted scrypt 
=== PAGE 23 ===
23 
hash (16-byte salt, 32-byte key, via node:crypto) rather than in plaintext, a user record 
is appended to the shared store and a bearer token is returned to the client. Login 
verifies the submitted password against the stored hash and issues a 192-bit random 
token with a 24-hour expiry; tokens are held in an in -memory session table, and 
expired tokens are rejected and pruned on use. The current -user route returns the 
current username when the request carries a valid, unexpired token. Logout deletes 
the token from the session table. Every route except /health, register and login 
requires the Authorization: Bearer <token> header, and requests without a valid token 
receive a 401 response. The web client stores the token obtained at login and attaches 
it to all subsequent API calls. Records created by earlier builds that stored plaintext 
passwords are upgraded to a scrypt hash on first successful login. The remaining 
security limitations of this prototype are reviewed in Chapter 4. 
3.5. Key Interaction Sequences 
The core usage flows are best described as short sequences of API calls rather 
than as screens: 
1. Worker browsing: the worker logs in, requests the ranked job deck ( GET 
/api/deck/worker/w001) and swipes right or left on each card; every swipe 
records a like or a pass for that worker-job pair (POST /api/swipes). 
2. Employer browsing: the employer logs in, requests the ranked worker deck 
(GET /api/deck/job/j001) and evaluates candidate cards against the score 
bars and reason lists. 
3. Mutual match: when the same worker -job pair has both a worker like and an 
employer like and no match exists yet, the swipe handler creates a Match 
record and returns it in the response, so the interface can immediately 
show the match banner and unlock the chat for both sides. 
4. Post-match messaging: with the match id, either side lists ( GET 
/api/chats/:matchId) or sends ( POST /api/chats/:matchId) messages; each 
message carries the sender, the text and a server timestamp. 
The dual-consent rule is enforced in exactly one place, the swipe handler, which 
checks that both like records exist before creating a match and refuses to duplicate an 
existing match. Contact details are not part of either profile type, so no private 
information changes hands before mutual interest has been established.

3.6. Seed Data and Cold-Start Rationale 
Two-sided markets face a chicken -and-egg problem at launch: employers see 
little value without candidates, and candidates see little value without jobs ( Rochet & 
Tirole, 2003; Schein et al., 2002). The prototype addresses this for development and 
evaluation with a deterministic, synthetic seed set rather than scraped or crowd -
sourced data. The seed contains 30 worker profiles and 20 job postings spread across 
the five trades and the ten Riga districts used by the prototype, with realistic ranges 
for experience (a few years up to over twenty), hourly rates and availability states. 
Worker profiles include a trade, a skill list, experience, a district, language tags ( lv, 
ru, en), availability and an hourly rate. Job postings include an employer, a title, 
required skills, a district, a pay band, a start state and a season. Because the seed is 
deterministic and versioned, every development run and every evaluation run 
observes the same market state, which makes the ranker output reproducible. The 
choice of 30 workers and 20 jobs balances breadth and audibility: the trades and 
districts are each well represented and the pool is small enough to inspect profile by 
profile. A market this small caps how much feedback a run can collect, so the 
controlled experiment of Chapter 5 does not rely on the pinned seed alone; it re -
generates the market structure under many replicate seeds at three sizes and reports 
intervals across markets rather than single-seed point estimates. 
3.7. User Interface Design and Interaction 
The web frontend provides a card -swiping interface. Workers view pre -ranked 
job cards displaying employer details, hourly rates, required skills and score 
breakdown bars. Employers view candidate cards displaying declared trade skills, 
years of experience and proximity. Swiping right records a like; if both parties 
express mutual interest, the API instantly creates a match state and unlocks direct 
messaging. The interface uses a dark theme with glassmorphism elements, score bars 
and reason lists for each recommendation. Authentication is implemented with token -
based sessions: users register or log in, receive a bearer token and include it in 
subsequent API requests. 
The interface is intentionally minimal. The dark theme reduces glare in the low -
light conditions in which tradespeople often plan their day, the score bars encode the 
interpretable factors in rank order, and the reason lists restate the ranker's plain -
=== PAGE 25 ===
25 
language output. The layout is a single -column deck with a thumb -reachable action 
zone, following the mobile-first constraint established earlier.

5.2. Baseline Comparison Protocol 
The active -learning ranker will be compared against a static fixed -weight 
baseline at feedback checkpoints (T = 0, 10, 25, 50, 75, 100 and 150 labelled pairs), 
=== PAGE 31 ===
31 
stated as H0 (no difference) versus H1 (active learning improves Precision@K and 
reduces task time). Ground -truth relevance labels are assigned by fixed rules 
documented in the test protocol (skill overlap ≥ 2 and pay gap ≤ 20% and availability 
matches); because the seed data is synthetic, the generator parameters will be 
published so that circularity can be inspected. Recruiters are simulated rule -following 
agents with 10% label noise unless real participants are recruited (target n = 5-8); 
results will be reported over multiple random seeds with confidence intervals. The 
metric set adds a rank -aware measure ( NDCG@K, plus MRR). A small bias audit is 
planned: score disparity across a synthetic protected attribute (for example region) 
before and after feedback. 
Data collection follows a pre -registered protocol. Each simulated recruiter run 
records, at every feedback checkpoint (T = 0, 10, 25, 50, 75, 100 and 150 labelled 
pairs), the full ranking produced by each ranker, the labels that the rule-based ground 
truth assigned, and the resulting metric values. The raw recorded rankings, not the 
aggregates, are archived so that any disagreement between a reported number and the 
raw output can be audited. If real participants are available (target n = 5-8), they 
repeat the same protocol in a supervised session, with task time measured from the 
first swipe until the participant confirms three shortlisted candidates. All runs are 
executed over multiple random seeds and reported with confidence intervals rather 
than point estimates. 
Analysis plan. The primary comparison is the active -learning ranker versus the 
fixed-weight baseline at each checkpoint. Precision@K and Recall@K are compared 
as means with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals over seeds. The median task 
completion time is compared with a paired sign test, because completion times are 
typically right -skewed. Match success, NDCG@K and MRR are reported 
descriptively in the same tables as the primary metrics. A difference is considered 
material only if it is both statistically distinguishable and of practical size (at least five 
percentage points in Precision@K), so that small but statistically significant effects on 
a synthetic market are not over-interpreted. 
Relation to the seed market. The evaluation consumes the deterministic seed 
market described in Section 3.6 (30 workers, 20 jobs, 10 districts). The layout is 
advantageous for the study: the trade strata are small enough that each stratum can be 
observed within a single run, so Precision@K can be computed per trade and 
averaged rather than over the whole pool. The same generator that produced

5.3. Threats to Validity 
Three threats are acknowledged. Internal validity: relevance labels are generated 
by rules, so the ranker could be rewarded for reproducing those rules; publishing the 
generator and using held -out seeds mitigates but does not remove this. External 
validity: the seed data is synthetic and small, so results demonstrate technical 
feasibility and interaction behaviour rather than labour -market outcomes. Construct 
validity: engagement and match success are proxies for hiring quality and must be 
reported as such. 
5.4. Ethical Considerations 
The study touches only synthetic data and voluntarily provided participant data. 
Participant data will be collected with informed consent, stored locally, and reported 
anonymously. The bias audit is included not as a formality but because the analytical 
chapter identified statistical discrimination as a structural risk of data -driven 
screening (Barocas & Selbst, 2016); its result will be reported regardless of whether it 
favours the proposed system. No experimental condition is designed to deceive 
participants, and the human -in-the-loop property is preserved in every test scenario, 
so no decision that a participant sees is produced autonomously. 
5.5. Reproducibility 
The evaluation is reproducible by design. The seed generator, the ranking code 
and the evaluation scripts are version -controlled together with the thesis, the seed 
market is deterministic (Section 3.6), and every recorded run logs the exact inputs and 
outputs; reports state the software versions and random seeds used. Any reviewer can 
therefore rerun the comparison locally without external services. 
5.6. Decision Criteria 
The outcome feeds a go/no-go decision for the final thesis rather than a pass or 
fail for the semester's work. The directional expectation, argued in Section 2.3, is that 
the feedback -updated weights outperform the fixed weights on held -out relevance 
judgements, with the improvement concentrated in the first few checkpoints. If the

recall loss beyond one point, the final thesis presents the active -learning result as the 
core contribution; if no stable improvement is found, the thesis becomes a rigorous 
negative result and presents the interpretable fixed -weight system as the contribution. 
Under both branches the deliverables of this semester - the prototype, the seed market 
and the evaluation harness - remain valid.

1. Literature synthesis: the review showed that skilled -trades hiring in Latvia 
leans heavily on informal networks and personal referrals ( Hoffman, 
2017), justifying a gesture-driven, mobile-first approach. 
2. Regulatory architecture: a compliant hybrid human -in-the-loop architecture 
was formulated that fulfils the transparency and oversight mandates of 
Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (European Parliament & Council, 2024a). 
3. Prototype verification: a three -tier system ( FastAPI, Express, React) 
supporting reciprocal swiping, multi -factor ranking and mutual -match 
messaging was built, and its builds, schema contracts, 13 API routes, 
scrypt password hashing and expiring session tokens were verified. 
4. Evaluation methodology and result: a six -metric evaluation methodology 
(Precision@K, Recall@K, match success, task time, NDCG@K, MRR) 
was established, voice and calendar extensions were de -scoped, and a 
preliminary controlled experiment showed  the safeguarded active learner 
improving Precision@2 by 6.9 and 5.7 percentage points on the two larger 
replicate markets and matching the fixed weights on the smallest. 
The next steps are to extend the controlled experiment to the regions left out of 
phase one, complete the verification -hardening plan (negative and concurrency cases, 
and automated pytest and supertest suites), run the final -stage comparison with any 
available participants, and write the results and discussion chapters of the final thesis. 
Taken together, this report demonstrates an end -to-end path from a clearly 
defined labour-market problem to a running prototype and an executed evaluation of 
its active -learning layer. The remaining semester's work is substantial but well 
scoped: extending the seed market to the regions left out of phase one, broadening the 
comparison to the full metric set and to real participants, and completing the 
hardening plan. The main risk is not technical feasibility, which the prototype, its 
verification and the preliminary experiment support, but the effect size that an 
interpretable ranker can deliver once the synthetic market is replaced by real 
feedback; for this reason the bias audit and the published seed generator will form 
part of the final write-up.
